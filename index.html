Lake Mungo: how do scientists date the past?

How old is the oldest evidence at Lake Mungo, and why did the answer keep changing?

What is agreed

Lake Mungo is one of the Willandra Lakes in south-western New South Wales. The lakes have been dry for thousands of years. Along the eastern shore of Lake Mungo is a long crescent-shaped dune, called a lunette, whose layers of sand and clay record changes in the lake. The Willandra Lakes Region was placed on the World Heritage List in 1981.

The region is the Country of the Barkindji (Paakantji), Ngiyampaa and Mutthi Mutthi peoples, who continue to have close connections to the Willandra Lakes.

In 1968 geologist Jim Bowler found human remains eroding from the lunette. They are known publicly as Mungo Lady. In 1974 he found the remains of a man, known publicly as Mungo Man. Scientists have also used the names Mungo I and Mungo III.

Different methods have given different ages. Early radiocarbon estimates placed Mungo Lady at about 20,000 to 26,000 years ago. Estimates for Mungo Man have included about 30,000 years, 42,000–45,000 years, and, in 1999, 62,000 years.

In 2003 a team led by Jim Bowler published 25 new optical (OSL) ages in the journal Nature. They concluded that both people lived about 40,000 years ago (give or take 2,000 years), and that people were at Lake Mungo by 50,000 to 46,000 years ago.

Mungo Lady was returned to Lake Mungo in 1992. Mungo Man was returned to Country in November 2017, with 103 other ancestral remains. In 2022 the Australian Government approved a proposal to rebury the ancestral remains on Country. Traditional Owners have not all agreed about how the ancestors should rest. This file does not take a side.

    Three witnesses

    The same three questions for each: What did they say they saw? · Could they really have seen it? · Why might they say it? A period portrait is used where one exists and is free to reuse. Otherwise you will see a plain silhouette, not a likeness. Tap a picture to enlarge it.

    Alan Thorne and colleagues

    Anthropologist and dating scientists · Australian National University and others · Journal of Human Evolution, June 1999 · Thorne died in 2012

    The team who dated Mungo Man directly, using uranium in the bones and teeth, and reached the oldest date of all.

    Source. A. Thorne, R. Grün, G. Mortimer, N. A. Spooner, J. J. Simpson, M. McCulloch, L. Taylor and D. Curnoe, “Australia’s oldest human remains: age of the Lake Mungo 3 skeleton,” Journal of Human Evolution 36, no. 6 (1999), pp. 591–612. Quoted from the published abstract. The image is a document silhouette, not a facsimile.

    What they said. “The best age estimate for the human skeleton is obtained through the combination of U-series and ESR analyses yielding 62,000 ± 6000 years.” They wrote that this was “in close agreement with OSL age estimates on the sediment … of 61,000 ± 2000 years,” and that all their results “are considerably older than the previously assumed age of LM3 and demonstrate the necessity for directly dating hominid remains.”

    Could they really have seen it? They measured the material themselves, with two methods (uranium-series and electron spin resonance) that can reach far beyond radiocarbon. But they also wrote that “some minor uranium mobilization may have occurred in the past”: uranium moving in or out of bone can change the answer. They did not find the remains; they worked in a laboratory, decades later.

    Why might they say it? To fix a date by testing the remains themselves rather than the sand around them. A very old date also supported their wider ideas about early human history. Students can ask: does dating the thing itself always beat dating what it was found in?

    Jim Bowler and colleagues

    Geologist who found both sets of remains, with dating scientists · Nature, 20 February 2003

    The geologist who knew the lunette best, returning with a large new set of sand dates.

    Source. J. M. Bowler, H. Johnston, J. M. Olley, J. R. Prescott, R. G. Roberts, W. Shawcross and N. A. Spooner, “New ages for human occupation and climatic change at Lake Mungo, Australia,” Nature 421 (2003), pp. 837–840. Quoted from the published abstract. The image is a document silhouette, not a facsimile.

    What they said. “Here we report a new series of 25 optical ages showing that both burials occurred at 40 ± 2 kyr ago and that humans were present at Lake Mungo by 50–46 kyr ago.” They wrote that “this new chronology corrects previous estimates for human burials at this important site.”

    Could they really have seen it? Bowler found both sets of remains and had studied the lunette’s layers for decades. The team dated the sand in many places and checked that the ages ran in order, oldest at the bottom. But OSL dates the sand, not the people. A date for the remains depends on reading correctly which layer they were placed in.

    Why might they say it? To correct earlier dates, including the 62,000-year result, with many samples instead of a few. The paper also links people at the lake to a changing climate. Students can ask whether 25 dates from the sand outweigh direct dates from the remains, or whether they answer a different question.

    National Museum of Australia

    National museum, Canberra · Defining Moments: “Mungo Lady” · Web page, updated 8 July 2025

    A public museum summarising the science for everyone, years after the arguments.

    Source. National Museum of Australia, “Mungo Lady,” Defining Moments, nma.gov.au/defining-moments/resources/mungo-lady (updated 8 July 2025). Quoted from the web page, which cites Bowler et al. (2003) among its references. The image is a document silhouette, not a facsimile.

    What they said. “We now know that the remains of Mungo Lady are 40,000 to 42,000 years old, making them the oldest human remains found anywhere in Australia.” “Over the decades, with the development of increasingly accurate archaeological dating techniques, scientists have dated the remains to between 40,000 and 42,000 years ago.” On the same page: “Humans arrived at the lake about 40,000 years ago, probably by following a river from the coast.”

    Could they really have seen it? The museum did not do the dating. It summarises other people’s research for the public, and it chooses which results to repeat. Its page also says “We now know”, which sounds more certain than the scientists sometimes are.

    Why might they say it? To give visitors and students a clear, reliable short version. A clear summary has to round numbers and leave out arguments. Students can compare “arrived at the lake about 40,000 years ago” with the 2003 paper’s “present at Lake Mungo by 50–46 kyr ago.”

    Why it still matters

    Lake Mungo changed what Australians knew about the deep past, and it changed how scientists date it. The file is open because each new method gave a new answer, and because a museum label has to choose one. Who decides what happens to ancestors, and what science may still ask of them, is a question about the present.

    Question. Which of the three numbers in the notebook is rounded the most, and does the rounding change the story?

    2. Four clocks

    Radiocarbon counts carbon-14 in once-living material, and stops working much beyond about 50,000 years. Uranium-series dating measures uranium and its decay products, which can soak into bone after burial. Electron spin resonance (ESR) measures radiation damage that builds up in tooth enamel. OSL measures energy stored in sand grains since they were last in sunlight.

    Question. For each clock, what exactly is being dated, and what could make it run fast or slow?

    3. Dating the remains, or the sand?

    The 1999 team argued for dating remains directly. The 2003 team dated the layers of the lunette in two lines, called transects, and checked that the ages were in order. Both methods need assumptions: about uranium moving, or about which layer the remains belong to.

    Question. Which assumption would you rather rely on, and why?

    4. A museum rounds the numbers

    The National Museum page says Mungo Lady is 40,000 to 42,000 years old, and that humans arrived at the lake about 40,000 years ago. The 2003 paper says people were present by 50,000 to 46,000 years ago. The museum also reports about 460 human footprints found near the lake in 2003, dated to about 20,000 years ago.

    Question. Is a museum wrong to round, or is it doing a different job from a scientific paper?

    5. Who decides about ancestors?

    Mungo Lady was returned in 1992 and Mungo Man in 2017. The Australian National University apologised to Elders for the hurt the removal caused. In 2022 the Australian Government approved a reburial proposal supported by the Willandra Lakes Region Aboriginal Advisory Group. Some Traditional Owners and some scientists wanted a keeping place instead. These are public facts; this file does not take a side.

    Question. Why might the people whose ancestors these are see the question “how old?” differently from the scientists who asked it?

    6. Sure, maybe, or a guess?

    Sure: remains were found at Lake Mungo in 1968 and 1974; different methods have given ages from about 20,000 to 62,000 years; the 2003 study gave about 40,000 years for both and placed people at the lake by 50,000–46,000 years ago; both have been returned to Country. Maybe: which date is closest to the truth. A guess: an exact year. The opener is not a source.

    Question. Write a finding of no more than five sentences, and mark each sentence sure, maybe, or a guess.
